0. How this tool thinks about your number

Most retirement calculators assume you'll spend 70–80% of today's income forever and call it done. This one instead asks: what does your actual life cost today — kids, parents, debt, healthcare, housing — and how does each of those pieces change as you get older? It adds those up year by year, in the currency and city you'll really be living in, until the age you tell it to plan to. A flat "4% withdrawal rule" is never used as the answer by itself. New here? Why RetireCompass works this way

Most retirement calculators reduce your entire future to one formula: assume you'll spend 70–80% of today's income forever, assume a 4% withdrawal rate, and call it a plan. That approach quietly erases the exact things that actually break real households' plans. This tool was built to put those things back in, as explicit, editable inputs rather than hidden assumptions.

A note on incentives

Many commercial retirement planning tools and advisors are paid a percentage of the assets you keep invested with them, or earn a commission when you buy a product. That's not automatically bad, but it does mean the tool has a built-in reason to make you feel like you never have quite enough — a permanently anxious client saves more, delays retiring, and keeps more money under management. This tool runs entirely on your own device, is free, and has no product to sell you, so it has no reason to do that. It's built to flag both directions honestly: not just "you're behind," but also "you may be saving more than you'll ever need, and could retire earlier or spend more on your health and your life now." See the roadmap and flags in your results for both kinds of finding.

The problems with a "one number" retirement calculator

  • Spending doesn't drop by a fixed percentage the day you retire. Some costs vanish (commuting), some barely change (housing), and some go up (healthcare, travel, hobbies). Averaging them into "75% of income" hides which specific costs you're actually counting on being lower.
  • Healthcare and housing don't inflate at the same rate as everything else. They usually run hotter than general inflation, for decades, and a plan that uses one blended inflation number for everything will systematically understate your real cost of living in your 70s and 80s.
  • Children don't become "independent" on a fixed schedule. Tuition, a coverage gap after graduation, or a period of family support are common, real, and often land during the exact years you're hoping to retire.
  • A credit card that only gets the minimum payment is not "debt you're paying off" — it's growing. A plan that just nets "debt payments" against income without checking whether a balance is actually shrinking will overstate how much progress is being made.
  • Job loss and disability risk are not evenly spread across a career. In most fields the real risk climbs later in a career, exactly when it's hardest to recover from and exactly when a plan is most sensitive to an income gap.
  • A single "you'll live to 85" assumption ignores the tail risk that actually ruins plans — living to 95 or 100, a long-term-care period, or the death of one partner leaving the other with less pension income but almost the same fixed costs.
  • Tax is not a flat percentage. It's progressive, it interacts with pension clawback and healthcare-premium surcharge rules in some countries, and ignoring that shape can make a withdrawal plan look more efficient than it really is.
  • A single, average investment return hides sequence-of-returns risk — the very real danger that a few bad years of returns right after you retire do far more damage than the same bad years spread evenly across a 40-year plan.

What this tool does instead

RetireCompass walks your household forward one year at a time, from today until the age you choose to plan to, and in every single year it recalculates: what your job/partner's job pays (and the chance either of you loses that income); what your kids, parents, pets, debt and insurance actually cost that specific year; what your pensions and investments provide; what tax you'd owe on all of that; and whether your accounts grew or shrank as a result. It reports the first year — if any — that the household runs out of money, plus a second "stress" run that assumes a worse decade of investment returns and faster healthcare inflation, so you can see not just whether the base case works but how much margin you actually have.

What "stylized" means, and why some numbers here are deliberately simplified

Tax brackets, pension clawback thresholds, healthcare-premium surcharges and country contribution-room figures in this tool are simplified planning anchors, not a real tax filing. Building an exact tax engine for every country would take this tool well beyond what a single household needs — the goal here is to show the correct shape of tax (progressive, with a clawback cliff where relevant) rather than pretend a single flat percentage is realistic. Wherever it matters, replace the built-in numbers with your own, from a real tax return, a pension statement, or an insurance quote.

How to use RetireCompass, section by section

Work through the "Plan" tab from section 1 to section 13, in order — each section builds on the ones before it. Section 14 (Your result) and section 15 (Year-by-year detail) are where the tool answers you back; you don't type anything there. Click Calculate any time to refresh the results — the year-by-year table, KPIs, flags and report all update together.

Step-by-step

  1. Section 1 — You, your household, and your money's currency. Set your ages, country, and city cost band. If you plan to retire somewhere that uses a different currency from where your investments are held, fill in the spend-currency and exchange-rate fields — otherwise leave them blank. Pick a "need rule": most households should start with "only use the specific costs you type in below" (the buckets approach) and try "assume you'll keep spending like your current take-home pay" as a sanity check afterward.
  2. Section 2 — Your job. Choosing a profession loads a starting cost-of-living multiplier and job-loss risk curve for that field — read the note underneath, then adjust the numbers if your own situation is different (a specific employer, a specialized niche, a union role, etc.). Only turn on the "guaranteed" job-loss or disability shocks if you want to test a specific, deliberate scenario (e.g. "what if I'm let go at 58") — leave them off for your baseline plan.
  3. Section 3 — Partner. If you have a partner, fill in their numbers. The "survivor path" is optional and models one of you passing away before the other — useful for checking that the surviving partner isn't left short, but not required for a first pass.
  4. Sections 4–7 — Kids, parents, gifts, pets. Add one row per child, and per pet. These are usually the categories people forget to plan for, or plan for only until a fixed "independence" age that doesn't match reality (adult children needing help, aging parents needing support).
  5. Section 8 — Debt. List every mortgage, credit card, line of credit, and loan with its real balance, interest rate and payment. If the tool flags a card as "not amortizing," that specific balance is a priority to fix before optimizing anything else — see "What to do next" in your report.
  6. Section 9 — Insurance. Fill in what you actually pay and what you'd actually receive — insurance is only useful in the plan if it's tied to a real policy number.
  7. Section 10 — Home sale. Leave this off unless you have a real, reasonably concrete plan to sell or downsize.
  8. Section 11 — Accounts. Enter your actual combined balance and what you intend to contribute each year. The note under this section will tell you if your intended contributions look larger than what's typically tax-sheltered in your country.
  9. Section 12 — Taxes. Start with "use this country's typical tax bands" — it's more realistic than a flat rate for most households, especially once pensions and withdrawals stack on top of other income.
  10. Section 13 — Everyday living, pensions, LTC. This is where most of your actual dollar amounts live: what your life costs today, and what your pensions pay. Take the time to get these numbers close to your real bank/credit-card statements — the rest of the tool can only be as accurate as this section.
  11. Click Calculate, then read section 14 top to bottom. Start with the verdict sentence, then the KPIs, then the flags (each flag is a specific, actionable finding), then the expense-mix table for the year you stop working.
  12. Use Print / PDF to save a copy. The generated report includes every input you entered, organized by section, plus your results and a "what to do next" list — so you (or an advisor) can review the whole plan later without having to open the tool again.

A sensible order to iterate in

First pass: fill in your real numbers everywhere and leave every "shock" toggle (job-loss shock, disability shock, survivor path) off. This is your base case. Second pass: turn on the survivor path and see if the surviving partner is still funded. Third pass: turn on a job-loss or disability shock at an age you're genuinely worried about, and see how much it moves the "money runs out" age. Treat the stress-test KPI (mentioned in the verdict) the same way — it's telling you your margin, not predicting the future.

Building safety and passive income as retirement gets closer

This tool doesn't manage your investments, and this section is educational background, not personal investment advice — for that, talk to a licensed advisor and read a real prospectus before buying anything. But the plan above assumes a return rate (sections 11 and 13) that quietly implies an investment strategy, and most people never actually revisit that strategy as they get closer to needing the money. The core idea worth knowing: the closer you are to needing money, the less you can afford for that money to be volatile — so a typical, sensible pattern is to hold more in growth assets (stocks) while retirement is decades away, then gradually shift toward steadier, income-producing assets as it approaches, and keep an actual cash buffer once you're drawing from it.

Your emergency fund is not your investment portfolio

A house repair, a car repair, or a job gap shouldn't ever force you to sell investments at a bad moment, or reach for a high-APR credit card — that's exactly what an emergency fund is for. It belongs in a plain savings account, not invested, and how much you need in it reasonably grows as you get older and your life gets more expensive to interrupt. This tool's "Emergency fund balance today" (section 11) is compared against the age-based target below, and any gap shows up in your flags and "what to do next."

How to actually make this succeed

A realistic plan fails for boring, fixable reasons far more often than it fails because of the stock market. In roughly the order they matter:

  1. Pay off high-APR debt before you invest more. A credit card at 20% APR is a guaranteed 20% "return" on paying it down — no legitimate investment reliably beats that. Section 8's non-amortizing-card flag exists because of exactly this.
  2. Automate it — pay yourself first. Set your savings/investment contribution to move automatically the day you're paid, before it can become discretionary spending. Money that has to be manually moved every month gets skipped in a busy month; money that moves automatically doesn't.
  3. Audit recurring subscriptions once or twice a year. Streaming services, apps, memberships, and subscription boxes are individually small and collectively easy to lose track of. As a worked example: three $15/month subscriptions you're not really using is $45/month — redirected into an investment account for 20 years at a 6% return, that's roughly $21,000, from money you likely wouldn't miss today.
  4. Redirect raises before you get used to them. When your income rises, increase your savings rate by at least half of the raise before your lifestyle absorbs all of it — this is the single biggest lever most people never pull.
  5. Keep the emergency fund separate and untouched for its actual purpose. If it becomes the "vacation fund" it stops being an emergency fund, and the next real emergency goes on a credit card instead.
  6. Don't try to time the market, and don't panic-sell in a downturn. The stress test in this tool exists so a bad decade is already priced into your plan — reacting to it by selling low turns a modeled risk into a real, permanent loss.
  7. Rebalance roughly once a year back toward the allocation you intended (see the glide-path table above), rather than letting whichever asset did best lately quietly take over your risk level.
  8. Prefer low-fee, broadly diversified funds over picking individual stocks or paying for actively managed funds — the "MER / fee drag" input in section 11 is a direct, compounding cost, and lower is almost always better, all else equal.

Where passive income typically comes from

  • Broad index mutual funds / ETFs. Low-cost, diversified exposure to stocks or bonds without picking individual companies — usually the core building block for most of a portfolio, at any age. The "MER / fee drag" input in section 11 is exactly this: index funds tend to have a much lower fee than actively managed funds, and that difference compounds over decades.
  • Dividend-paying stocks or dividend-focused funds. Shares in companies that regularly pay out a portion of profit to shareholders — a source of cash income you can spend without necessarily selling the underlying shares, though dividends aren't guaranteed and can be cut.
  • Bonds and GICs / term deposits. You lend money (to a government, a company, or a bank) for a set return, generally steadier and less volatile than stocks, and typically the "ballast" that increases as a glide path gets closer to retirement.
  • REITs (real estate investment trusts). A way to get rental-property-style income and exposure without directly owning and managing a building yourself.
  • Precious metals (e.g., gold). Usually held as a small diversifier (commonly cited examples are in the 5–10% range) rather than a core holding — it doesn't pay income and its price can be volatile too, but it doesn't move in the same direction as stocks and bonds in every environment, which is the main reason people hold a little.
  • An emergency/cash buffer. Not really an "investment," but the thing that keeps a bad year from turning into a forced bad decision — this is what the last row of the glide path table above is really describing.

Glossary — every term in this tool, in plain language

Terms are grouped by the section they appear in. If you're not sure what an input means, you can also just hover the label text in the form itself — most labels already explain themselves with an example in parentheses.

General

Stylized
Simplified for planning purposes — the shape of a real-world rule (like progressive tax, or pension clawback), not the exact legal formula. Always double check anything stylized against your real tax return, pension statement, or insurance policy.
Base case
Your plan using the "normal", expected numbers you typed in — no bad-luck scenario applied.
Stress test
The same plan, but assuming worse investment returns for your first 10 years of retirement and faster healthcare inflation the whole way through. This tells you your margin of safety, not a prediction.
Sequence-of-returns risk
The danger that a few bad investment years right after you stop working do far more damage than the same bad years spread evenly across your whole retirement — because you're withdrawing money at the same time the balance is down.
Withdrawal, "grossed up"
When you withdraw money from an account to cover a cost, you may also owe tax on that withdrawal. "Grossing up" means withdrawing a bit extra so that after tax, you still have enough left to cover the actual cost.

Investing basics (see the "Investing basics" tab for the full explanation)

Glide path
The general idea of gradually shifting a portfolio from more growth-oriented assets (stocks) toward steadier, income-producing ones (bonds, cash) as you get closer to needing the money.
Dividend
A cash payment some companies make to shareholders out of profit — a source of income from stocks without necessarily selling them. Not guaranteed, and can be reduced or stopped.
REIT
Real estate investment trust — a way to invest in rental-style real estate income through a fund, rather than buying and managing a property directly.
GIC / term deposit
A guaranteed-return account for a fixed term, generally steadier than stocks — used as "ballast" in a portfolio, more of it as retirement gets closer.
Precious metals
Physical assets like gold, sometimes held as a small diversifier because they don't reliably move in the same direction as stocks and bonds — not an income-producing asset on its own.
Emergency fund
Cash kept in a plain savings account — not invested — specifically to cover a car repair, a home repair, or a job gap without having to sell investments at a bad time or reach for high-APR debt. The target amount grows as you get older, per the table in "Investing basics."

Section 1 — Household & currency

City cost band
A multiplier applied to your everyday and discretionary spending based on how expensive the city you'll retire in is. It does not change your housing or healthcare numbers — those should already reflect your specific city.
Need rule
How the tool decides what you need each year: only the specific costs you typed in ("buckets"), a number based on your current take-home pay ("salary path"), or whichever of the two is higher.
Lifestyle inflation
How much your everyday spending tends to grow each year beyond general inflation — people's living standards usually creep up faster than the official inflation rate.
Exchange rate / FX drift
Only relevant if you'll spend money in a different currency than your investment accounts are held in. "FX drift" is your guess at how that exchange rate will change over time.

Section 2 — Job, layoff risk, disability

Job-loss hazard
Your estimated yearly chance of losing your job. In this tool it's modeled as low early in a career, then climbing after a "turning-point age" — most fields see rising risk with age, not constant risk.
Turning-point age
The age this tool assumes your job-loss risk starts climbing for your profession.
Hazard cap
The most that yearly risk can ever reach in the model — a ceiling, so the number never becomes unrealistic.
Guaranteed shock
A one-time, deliberate "what if" event you add on top of the yearly risk — e.g. "what if I'm definitely laid off at 58 for 2 years" — used to stress-test a specific worry, not part of your everyday baseline.

Section 3 — Partner & survivor

Cost-of-living stay-factor
The share of household costs that remain once only one of you is still earning. Most of a household's fixed costs (the house, insurance, one car) don't disappear just because one income does.
Survivor path
An optional scenario where one partner is assumed to pass away at a chosen age, so you can check whether the survivor still has enough — with less pension income but nearly the same living costs.
Pension / cost-of-living "keep-factor"
The share of a pension, or of household costs, that continues for the surviving partner after the other passes away.

Sections 4–7 — Kids, parents, gifts, pets

Independence age
The age you assume a child stops being a day-to-day financial dependent (though tuition and "after-help" can still continue past this age).
Education program preset
A typical tuition-and-duration package for a type of education (public college at home, public college away, private college, business school, medical school, law school, graduate school, trade certificate). These are stylized planning anchors, not your specific school's actual cost — edit the tuition/dates after picking one if you know your real numbers.
Registered education plan
A tax-advantaged account for education savings (RESP in Canada, 529 in the US, Junior ISA in the UK, and similar in other countries) — often with government matching money on top of your own contributions, which is why the tool flags it if you have tuition costs but aren't using one.
Education pot
A dedicated education savings account (RESP, 529 or similar). Tuition costs are paid from this account first; only the amount it can't cover shows up as a hit to your regular household cashflow.
Eldercare
Money you pay to support a parent, modeled as continuing until a chosen parent age (their expected end of that support, whether that's full facility care or passing).

Section 16 — Your roadmap

Checkpoint
A specific age the roadmap calls out, either because five years have passed since the last checkpoint, or because something in your plan actually changes that year (tuition starting/ending, a debt getting paid off, a modeled shock beginning or ending, and so on).
On-track / ahead / behind
A rough comparison between your projected account balance at that checkpoint and a simple straight-line target from today's balance to what you'll need at your stop-work age. It's a directional signal, not a precise verdict — small gaps are normal and not a reason to panic.

Section 8 — Debt

APR
Annual Percentage Rate — the yearly interest rate charged on a debt's balance (0.045 = 4.5% per year).
Amortizing
A debt is "amortizing" if your yearly payment is more than that year's interest, so the balance actually shrinks over time. If a payment is less than or equal to the interest, the balance grows instead — the tool flags this.

Sections 11–13 — Accounts, tax, living costs

MER (fee drag)
The yearly percentage your investment provider charges in fees, which reduces your actual investment return by that amount every year.
Contribution room
How much you're typically allowed to put into a tax-sheltered account per year in a given country. Money beyond this room usually goes into an ordinary taxable account instead, which is taxed differently.
Progressive tax bands
A tax system where your income is taxed in slices, at increasing rates as each slice is crossed — not one flat rate on your whole income.
Pension clawback
A rule (used in some countries, e.g. Canada's OAS) where your public pension is reduced once your income passes a threshold.
Healthcare-premium surcharge (IRMAA-style)
A rule (used in the US, tied to Medicare) where your healthcare premiums increase once your income crosses certain thresholds.
Long-term care (LTC)
A period of full-time paid care later in life — typically the largest and least predictable healthcare-related cost a household can face.

Section 14 — Your result

Verdict
A one-sentence plain-English summary of whether your plan holds up, and the single biggest reason if it doesn't.
Flags
Specific, individually actionable problems the simulation noticed — e.g. a non-amortizing credit card, or a child's costs still running after you retire.
Money runs out at age
The first age, after both of you have stopped working, that your account balance would reach zero under the base case.
